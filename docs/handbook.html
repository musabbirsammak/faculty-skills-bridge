Handbook · version 1

Faculty Skills Bridge

Open-source software that helps a university and its teachers move through AI-era curriculum change together: teachers see where their skills can take them, and the university plans courses, policy and training with the people affected.

For teachersFor heads, deans and leadershipFor administratorsAGPL-3.0

Chapter 1

What it is and why

When courses are retired or reshaped, the teachers who taught them are often left without a plan. Faculty Skills Bridge treats the university as one system: students, teachers and courses change together. It does three things.

Skills to course paths

Teachers add their skills and see which courses they could teach now, soon, or with a longer runway, and what to learn.

Work together

Policy proposals and a course redesign studio where colleagues edit, comment and decide together.

Insights

Totals for heads, deans and leadership: what the university can teach, where it must grow, who needs support.

Principles

  • Private by default. A teacher's skills, suggestions and feedback are visible only to that teacher.
  • Totals, not individuals. Leaders see aggregates, and small numbers that could point to a person are hidden.
  • Built by its users. Teachers add courses and skills, rate suggestions and co-write documents.
  • Explainable. Every number can be worked out by hand from the rules in chapter 11.

Each university runs its own copy on its own domain. The product name stays the same; the university's name appears beneath it.

Chapter 3

Getting started

  1. Create an account with your university email. Some universities only accept their own email domain, and some activate new accounts by hand, in which case you can log in once an administrator approves you.
  2. Choose your department in your profile. It decides which department totals you see.
  3. Set your main field (for example, Information & communication technologies, or Business, administration & law). Course paths outside it are then marked "Outside your field".
  4. Read "What can others see?", linked in the footer and the menu.
  5. Add your skills (chapter 4), then open Course paths (chapter 5).

Forgot your password? Use the link on the login page; a reset link is sent to your email.

Notifications

The bell next to your name shows how many notifications you haven't read. You are notified when something you are involved in changes: a course you suggested, edited, voted on or proposed changes to, and a proposal or redesign you started, edited, commented on or took a stance on. Notifications cover new comments and replies, edited sections, new stages, status decisions, proposed and decided course changes, and votes on your suggestions. Heads, deans and leaders are also notified of new course suggestions and proposed changes they are responsible for. Notifications never reveal who posted anonymously. You never get a notification for your own action. A university can also turn on email notifications.

Chapter 4

My skills

Add skills by typing (search also matches other names, so "docker" finds Containers (Docker, Kubernetes)) or by browsing by area. For each skill, choose a level.

LevelUse it whenCounts as
Can teach itYou could teach a course on it tomorrow, or have done so100%
Use it regularlyYou use it in your work, research or projects70%
FamiliarYou know the basics or used it a while ago35%

Rating well

  • Think beyond your course titles: tools, methods, research and industry work all count.
  • Be honest in both directions. Underrating hides real options; overrating suggests paths that will disappoint.
  • Missing a skill? Use Propose a new skill. Give it the name people in your field use, plus other names it goes by.
  • Update your skills after training, a project or a new course.

Chapter 6

Exploring a new area

Some teachers want to move into a completely different field. On Course paths, choose a field under Explore a new area. You see every course in that field, closest to your skills first, with no cut-off. Courses far from your skills get a longer path: an introductory course first, sitting in for a semester, and more than a year before teaching alone.

The side panel becomes Where to start: the missing skills that would bring the most high-impact courses in that field within reach.

Chapter 7

The catalogue

The catalogue lists every course and the skills it needs. Filter by search, field, area, status and recent subjects. Each card shows your match.

What a course records

ItemMeaning
AreaThe UNESCO ISCED-F 2013 detailed field that best describes the content (e.g. 0613 Software and applications development and analysis)
FieldThe broad ISCED-F field, set automatically from the area
Recent subjectA subject departments are adding in the AI era or for sustainability
Impact (1–5)Expected value of the subject over the next five years, with a reason and a source
SkillsCore skills are essential and count three times as much; supporting skills help
Status and departmentWhether the university offers, plans or is retiring it, and which department is responsible (chapter 8)

Adding or editing a course

  1. Open Catalogue → Add a course, or Edit course on a course page.
  2. Choose the ISCED-F area that matches the content, not the department that teaches it.
  3. Add the skills a teacher needs; tick Core for the essential ones (at least one).
  4. Rate impact and explain why in one sentence; name the source.

A new course starts as Suggested.

Changes are reviewed

  • Heads, deans and leaders responsible for a course edit it directly.
  • Everyone else clicks Propose changes. The proposal records only what changes, with an optional reason, and goes to the responsible heads, deans and leaders.
  • They see a before-and-after comparison under Course reviews and accept or decline it, with an optional note. The proposer and others involved are notified.
  • A course with changes waiting shows a notice at the top of its page.

Chapter 8

Course status decisions

Each course has a status at your university.

In the catalogue→ Suggested→ Planned→ Offered here→ Retiring

Status can move in any direction; the arrows show the usual life of a course.

StatusMeaning
In the catalogueA known subject the university hasn't decided on. All starter courses begin here
SuggestedSomeone thinks the university should teach it
PlannedThe university plans to start it
Offered hereTaught now
RetiringBeing phased out; its teachers may need a transition plan

Who decides: heads for their department's courses, deans for their faculty's, leadership for all; heads and deans can also decide courses not yet assigned to a department. Teachers cannot change status. On the course page, the Status here box takes a status, the responsible department and an optional reason. Every change is kept in a history: who, when, why.

Supporting or opposing a suggestion

While a course is Suggested, anyone can click Support or Oppose on its page (click again to withdraw). Only totals are shown, also in the Insights panel Suggested by teachers, so deciders can see how much backing an idea has. Voting closes when the status changes.

Status and proposals work togetherUse a policy proposal to argue for a new course or a retirement; once it is adopted, a head, dean or leader records the decision as a status.

Chapter 9

Policy proposals and the course redesign studio

Both work the same way. A proposal starts with the sections Problem, Proposal, Who is affected, How it would work, and Risks and open questions. A redesign starts from a catalogue course with Why redesign, Learning outcomes, Weekly topics, Assessment, AI use policy, and Skills a teacher needs (filled in from the course). Each section shows a guiding prompt until someone writes it.

Editing

  • Anyone can edit any section, and anyone can add a section.
  • Every save is kept. History shows each version, with added lines in green and removed lines in red.
  • If someone saved the same section while you were editing, you see their version instead of overwriting it; your text stays in the editor so you can merge.

Discussing

  • Comments are tagged Suggestion Support Concern Question and can be about one section or the whole document.
  • Replies stay one level deep so threads remain readable.
  • Post anonymously shows "A colleague" instead of your name, so anyone can raise a concern safely.
  • The document's author, the comment's author or an administrator can mark a comment as addressed.

Deciding

  • Stances: I support this / Support with changes / I have concerns. Only totals are shown; click again to withdraw.
  • Stages: Drafting → Open for discussion → Submitted to department → Adopted, or Closed. The author or an administrator changes the stage.
  • Each document can belong to a department or the whole university.

Chapter 11

How the numbers are calculated

The method is deliberately simple so anyone can check it.

1. Credit for each skill a course needs

Your situationCredit
Can teach it1.00
Use it regularly0.70
Familiar0.35
You don't have it, but have a related skill0.35 × that skill's credit (at most 0.35)
Neither0

If both apply, the higher credit counts. A skill with credit 0.60 or more counts as "You bring".

2. Match

Core skills count 3 times, supporting skills once.

match = (sum of weight × credit) ÷ (sum of weights)

Ready now: 70% or more and every core skill at 0.60 or more. Short preparation: 45% or more. Stretch: 25% or more.

3. Preparation time

Each skill below 0.60 adds the part still missing, weighted:

preparation = sum of weight × (1 − credit), for skills below 0.60
PreparationLabel
0ready now
up to 2.5a few weeks
up to 6about one semester
more than 6two semesters or more

Worked example

A software-testing teacher (Software testing, Test automation and Java at "can teach it") looks at Testing & Red-Teaming AI Systems.

SkillRoleWeightTeacher hasCreditWeight × credit
Model evaluationCore3Related: Software testing0.351.05
Software testingCore3Can teach it1.003.00
LLMs & prompt engineeringSupporting1No00
Test automationSupporting1Can teach it1.001.00
Penetration testingSupporting1Related: Software testing0.350.35
Ethics in computingSupporting1No00
Total105.40

Match = 5.40 ÷ 10 = 54%, so short preparation. Preparation = 3×0.65 + 1 + 0.65 + 1 = 4.6, so about one semester.

4. Skills that open the most doors

For each skill you're missing (credit below 0.60) in your short-preparation and stretch courses, the app adds impact × weight × (1 − credit) and shows the top five. In Explore, courses below the stretch threshold count too.

5. Shared training needs

For every teacher in the unit, and every recent subject in their short-preparation or stretch groups, each missing skill gets points:

The skill is…Teacher doesn't know itTeacher knows it a little
Core to the course3 points2 points
Supporting1 point0.65 points

Knowing only a related skill reduces the points a little. Points are added over all teachers; the eight skills with most points are shown, each bar relative to the top one. Unlike your personal list, this is not weighted by impact.

6. Privacy thresholds

  • Counts from 1 to 2 show as "<3" (the minimum, PRIVACY_MIN_GROUP, defaults to 3).
  • "Makes sense" shares and feedback totals appear only once 3 or more have answered.
  • Training needs, coverage gaps and unit details appear only when 3 or more teachers in that unit have added skills.

Chapter 12

Impact ratings

Impact estimates how much a subject's value will grow over the next five years. Where possible, starter ratings are anchored to the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025, which names AI and big data, networks and cybersecurity, and technological literacy as the fastest-growing skill areas to 2030, with environmental stewardship newly in the top ten.

RatingMeaningExamples
5Central to the fastest-growing areasMachine Learning, MLOps, Data Engineering, Information Security, Business Analytics
4Closely tied to a growing area, or a foundation for oneDeep Learning, Computer Networks, Probability & Statistics, Digital Marketing
3Stable, foundational or more specialisedData Structures, Operating Systems, Financial Accounting

No starter course is rated 1 or 2: nobody's subject should be labelled low-value without evidence. Ratings without a direct source are marked "Starter rating; review locally". The WEF data is global and about skills and jobs, not courses or any one country, so each university should review the ratings (chapter 17).

Chapter 13

Data and classification

Starter dataSkillsCoursesOf which recent
Computing833822
Business232610
Total1066432
  • How it was built: by hand, as an informed starting point. Course names follow the ACM/IEEE curriculum guidelines (CS2023 for computing); course–skill links reflect what teaching each course usually needs. Not measured from data.
  • Areas and fields: UNESCO ISCED-F 2013, the classification UNESCO, the OECD and Eurostat use. Courses are classified by content, so statistics sits under 0542 Statistics whichever department teaches it; mixed subjects use the inter-disciplinary codes (0688, 0488).
  • Skills: one standard name plus other names, the same structure as the EU's ESCO classification. Administrators can import and link ESCO skills with import_esco.
  • Skill families: groups for browsing only, chosen by hand; they don't affect matching.
  • Related skills: one or two per skill, chosen by judgement; they give partial credit.

Chapter 14

Best practices

Teachers

  • Spend ten honest minutes on your skills; revisit them each semester.
  • Answer "Does this fit you?" on your top suggestions. It costs a click and improves the catalogue.
  • Treat suggestions as a conversation starter with your head, not a verdict.
  • Use anonymous comments for real concerns, not for attacks.

Heads and deans

  • Record decisions as statuses with a reason, so teachers learn early what is planned or retiring.
  • For every retiring course, check Insights and agree a transition plan with the affected colleagues: target course, skills, a co-teaching semester, support.
  • Turn shared training needs into workshops and pair teachers for co-teaching.
  • Never ask teachers to show you their profiles. The system's value depends on that trust.

Leadership

  • Use proposals for curriculum decisions so affected staff have a voice before, not after.
  • Watch participation first: totals mean little until most teachers take part.
  • Compare coverage gaps with planned courses before approving new programmes.

Administrators

  • Keep the privacy minimum at 3 or higher, and publish who can access the server.
  • Set the email domain and, if needed, approval before announcing the site.
  • Back up the database regularly and keep copies off the server.

Chapter 15

Administration

Open /admin with an administrator account.

  1. Institution: name, short name, logo URL, allowed email domains, approval of new accounts, contact email. The name in .env is used only until this is saved.
  2. Faculties and departments: add each faculty with its departments.
  3. Users: set roles (head and dean need a department; a dean sees that department's faculty). Activate accounts waiting for approval with the "Activate selected accounts" action.
  4. Catalogue: load starter data with seed_catalog (and --file catalog/data/seed_business.json); add disciplines as new data files; import ESCO skills with import_esco.
  5. Demo: seed_demo creates demo faculties, teachers, a head, a dean and a VC; seed_demo --remove deletes them.

Installation, HTTPS, updates and backups are covered in docs/INSTALL.md.

Chapter 16

Limits

  • The starter catalogue and impact ratings are expert judgement, not yet reviewed by faculty or checked against real syllabi or local job data.
  • Matching relies on self-rated skills and on the catalogue's course–skill links; knowing a skill is not the same as being ready to teach it.
  • A course has one status and one responsible department, so a subject taught by several departments needs a separate course per department for now.
  • Notifications appear in the app; email notifications depend on the university's email settings.
  • Fields of study use only ISCED-F broad and detailed levels; each course has one area.
  • The interface is in English only.

Chapter 17

Next steps

For a university starting out

  1. Pilot with one or two departments and 10–20 teachers for one semester.
  2. Review the catalogue with 5–8 colleagues per field in two rounds: they rate impact and check skill links, see the first round's results, then rate again.
  3. Check against reality: tag your own syllabi with skills; count skills in 100–200 local job postings.
  4. Measure: participation, the share of suggestions rated "Makes sense", training delivered, transition plans agreed for retiring courses.
  5. Scale to other faculties, adding their disciplines as data files with their own staff.

For the software

  • A status per department, for subjects such as statistics that many departments teach.
  • Notification preferences (which events, and email digests).
  • Linking every skill to ESCO and importing ESCO's lecturer occupations as profile starters.
  • Bengali and other interface languages.
  • Comparing anonymised catalogues across universities.

Chapter 18

Glossary

Area
The ISCED-F 2013 detailed field of a course, e.g. 0411 Accounting and taxation.
Core skill
A skill essential to teach a course; counts three times as much as a supporting skill.
Coverage gap
A recent subject nobody in a unit is ready to teach.
Credit
How much of a skill a teacher has, from 0 to 1.
ESCO
The EU's multilingual classification of skills and occupations.
Field of study
The ISCED-F broad field, e.g. Business, administration & law.
Head start
A skill known a little, or reachable through a related skill.
Impact
A 1–5 rating of a subject's expected value over the next five years.
ISCED-F 2013
UNESCO's international classification of fields of education and training.
Match
The weighted share of a course's skills a teacher already covers.
Proposed change
A course edit by someone not responsible for the course, waiting for a head, dean or leader to accept it.
Privacy minimum
The smallest number shown in totals (default 3); smaller numbers are hidden.
Recent subject
A subject departments are adding in the AI era or for sustainability.
Related skill
A close skill that gives partial credit.
Scope
The unit whose totals you are viewing: university, faculty or department.
Status
Whether the university offers, plans, is retiring or has been asked to teach a course.
Tier
Ready now, short preparation or stretch.
Vote
Support or opposition for a suggested course; only totals are shown.
